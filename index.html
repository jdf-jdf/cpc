AI Basics

A working session for CPC staff

Presenter
Jo Flores
Open by saying what this is not: not a policy announcement, not a tool rollout, not a decision made somewhere else. It is an hour to get hands on the thing and form your own opinion. This deck is built for a small group and a conversation. Four places are marked for discussion — the icebreaker, and three "Ask the room" prompts. In a room of eight, those are the parts worth protecting when you run short on time; the slides can be skimmed, the conversation cannot be recovered later. What this hour is

We will

  • Explain what these tools are, in plain English
  • Talk about what we want to protect
  • Sort your actual work into what is safe and what is not
  • Try it on a real task you brought

We will not

  • Announce a policy or a tool CPC has picked
  • Tell you AI will fix your workload
  • Ask you to use it with client information
  • Get technical
The right column matters as much as the left. Staff walk into sessions like this assuming a decision has already been made and they are being managed into it. Say plainly that no tool has been picked and nothing changes tomorrow because of this hour.

Over to you

What is the most embarrassing thing you have used AI for?

To start it off: a birthday message. Getting out of plans. Naming a pet. Arguing with a landlord. Apologizing to someone.

You go first, and make it genuinely a little embarrassing. If you do not, nobody will. What this is actually for: almost everyone in the room is already using these tools and almost nobody has said so out loud at work. Once that is in the open, the rest of the hour is a conversation between people who all use it, rather than a lecture at people who supposedly do not. If you want a quick read on the room first, ask for hands: never used one, tried it once, use it now and then, use it most weeks. Twenty seconds, and it tells you whether to slow down on the basics or move through them. If the room is quiet, offer two or three of your own until someone bites. In a small group this usually takes one. Watch for the person who admits something that breaks the red-light rule later in the deck — pasting a client's message, uploading a document. Do not make an example of them. Thank them, move on, and let slide 14 do the work. If you embarrass the one person honest enough to say it, nobody tells you anything for the rest of the session, and you lose the only real information you were going to get.

What is a chatbot?

You type a question. It types back an answer. That is the whole thing.

Underneath, it is guessing what words come next — based on having read an enormous amount of writing. So it is not:

  • searching the internet
  • looking in a database
  • connected to CPC's files
  • checking whether it is right
Keep this short and land the one idea: it predicts text. Everything on the next few slides follows from it. If someone asks whether it is intelligent or conscious: you do not need to settle that to use the tool well. Move on. A useful line: extremely well read, extremely fast, and sometimes confidently wrong.

So it makes things up

Something that guesses the next words will guess even when it does not know. The made-up answer looks exactly like a correct one: same confidence, same tone, same neat formatting.

It is not lying. It does not know it is wrong.

Ask the room

Has one of these ever given you an answer you knew was wrong? What was it?

Worth a live example if you have one: ask it something specific about a small local organization or a niche rule and watch it produce a confident, wrong, well-formatted answer. That demo lands harder than the slide. Do not use a real client scenario for this. The discussion prompt usually surfaces something better than anything you could have prepared, because it comes from their own work. Let two or three people answer. Where this hurts most: names, dates, numbers, and rules. Which is to say, facts. Flag that it comes back on the budget line of the work map.

The whole session in one line

It drafts. You decide.

Nothing it writes reaches another person until you have read it.

Say it out loud and let it sit. Come back to it at the end. The framing that works with social services staff is professional judgment: this does not replace yours. It produces raw material for it, like a draft from a new intern — useful, never sent unread.

What we are protecting

  1. 1

    Our relationships

    Sitting with a community member is the work. A tool can draft the letter. It cannot be the conversation.

  2. 2

    Our thinking

    Judgment is the job. If you stop thinking about a case because something summarized it, you got worse at your work, not faster.

  3. 3

    Our inner lives

    What you type does not stay on the screen. Over time these tools shape what you come to believe.

  4. 4

    Our identities

    Faces, voices, and names can be faked convincingly now. Ours, and our community's.

  5. 5

    Our work

    The worry about being replaced is real. It is worth naming out loud, not managing around.

Five pillars, from the Center for Humane Technology. Everything after this slide — the rules, the lights, the checking — exists to protect them. It is not compliance.

This is the slide that gives the rest of the hour its reason. Without it, the traffic lights read as CPC covering itself. With it, they read as protecting the thing CPC is. The five pillars are the Center for Humane Technology's, from their 2026 report "Preserving What Makes Us Deeply Human in the Age of AI." Their originals: relationships, cognitive capacities, inner worlds, identities, work and contributions. The one-line translations here are ours, written for this room. Credit them — they are worth reading and the report is short. Their central argument, worth saying out loud: these harms are not accidents or growing pains. They come from business models that optimize for engagement and dependency, the same pattern that played out with social media. That framing usually changes how a room hears everything that follows. Pillar 1 is the one to dwell on for CPC. An organization whose entire value is human relationships with immigrant and low-income communities has more at stake here than a company selling software. Say that plainly. Pillar 5 is the one people are quietly worried about. Naming it costs you nothing and buys you the room's attention for the rest of the hour. Do not promise anything you cannot promise.

Over to you

Which of those five feels most at risk in your work?

Relationships · Thinking · Inner lives · Identities · Work

The best five minutes in the deck. Let it run. Expect relationships and work to dominate — those are the two that touch CPC staff most directly. If someone raises thinking, that is the sophisticated answer and worth drawing out: the risk is not that the tool is wrong, it is that you stop checking. Do not resolve the discussion. You are not here to reassure anyone. The honest position is that these are real risks, this room does not control most of them, and the part it does control is how CPC chooses to use the thing — which is the rest of the session. If the room is small enough, go around rather than taking volunteers. Everyone answers.

Two things to say out loud. First: this deck was drafted with Claude and the exercise later uses Claude. Say both plainly, here, before anyone works it out. It costs you nothing, and a slide that goes out of its way to be even-handed about four vendors is only worth something if the room knows which one you came in with. Second, and more useful: if CPC runs on Google Workspace or on Microsoft 365, there is very likely an AI already sitting inside the software staff open every morning. That is usually the easiest place to start, and it is a real question for whoever owns IT. Deliberately no pricing, model names, or benchmark scores. All three go out of date within weeks and none of them is why a nonprofit picks a tool. If someone asks which is smartest, the honest answer is that they leapfrog each other constantly and the difference does not show up in the work this room does. The account type matters far more than the brand: a free personal account and a paid organizational one can have very different terms about what happens to what you type. That is on the open-questions slide near the end.

Three ways you will meet it

  • 1

    Chat

    You type, it types back. One window. It cannot open your files or do anything on your computer.

    Everyone starts here

  • 2

    Cowork

    It works on your actual documents. Reads and writes files, connects to things like Google Drive, and carries a multi-step job through to a finished deliverable.

    Built for the work most of this room does

  • 3

    Code

    It works inside software projects, in a developer's terminal, writing and changing code.

    Built for engineers

Same underlying thing. The difference is how much it is allowed to touch — and the more it can touch, the more the rules later in this session matter.

The point of this slide is that "AI" is not one thing, and the version people have heard about — a chat window — is the least capable of the three. Cowork is Anthropic's product for knowledge work: research, document preparation, file work. It is the category that maps to what CPC program and admin staff actually do. Code is for engineers; mention it and move on so nobody thinks this is a coding session. Whether CPC would use any of these, and which, is an open question — say so.

The line that lands: you would not hire a PhD to alphabetize a list. For your own reference, Anthropic's current tiers run roughly Haiku (fast and cheap), Sonnet (the balanced default), Opus (heavy reasoning), and Fable (the most capable). Deliberately not on the slide — names change faster than a deck does, and this room does not need them. If someone asks which they should use: whichever the tool gives them by default is almost always fine to start.

You can also tell it how hard to think

Same helper. Two different instructions.

"Give me a quick answer"

  • Answers in seconds
  • Costs less
  • Fine for routine work
  • Thinner on hard problems

"Take your time and think"

  • Takes longer — sometimes minutes
  • Costs more
  • Catches more on complicated work
  • Can overthink something simple

Rule of thumb: quick for routine, careful for anything that matters. Most tools have a setting for this, or you can just ask in plain words.

This is the one genuinely new concept for people who have used a chatbot before, and it is worth the minute. Most people do not know the setting exists. In practice the two dials interact: a mid-tier helper told to think carefully often beats a top-tier one told to answer fast, and costs less. If someone asks how to choose, that is the honest answer. Do not let this become a technical detour. If questions get deep, take them offline.

Green light

Go ahead. No permission needed.

  • Rewriting something official into plain English
  • Summarizing a long document you never finished
  • A first draft of routine writing
  • Turning messy notes into a clean list
  • Brainstorming when you are stuck
  • Explaining jargon you ran into

None of these involve a real person's information.

Say explicitly that green means green — staff do not need to ask anyone before doing these. A training that only lists prohibitions teaches people the tool is a liability and they quietly use it anyway. Ask which of the six someone did this week. Usually several hands.

Yellow light

Use it, then check before it goes anywhere.

  • Anything with a date, number, or dollar amount
  • Anything about program rules or who qualifies
  • Translation — fine for gist, not for anything binding
  • Anything a client will read
  • Anything leaving CPC with our name on it
  • Anything you would sign your name to

The tool drafts. A person checks. Every time.

The checking method matters: verify at the source, not by asking the tool whether it is sure. Asked "are you sure?", it will apologize and produce a different answer with the same confidence. That is not verification. Translation gets pushback from anyone under deadline pressure, because the tool is genuinely useful there. Do not overclaim: rough pass then human review is legitimate; the human review is not optional. CPC's language-access obligations govern, not this slide.

Red light — the one rule to take out of this room

Client information never goes in

  • Names, addresses, phone numbers, dates of birth
  • Immigration or citizenship status
  • Case notes, or anything from a file
  • Benefits, income, or household details
  • Medical or mental health information
  • Photographs of documents

If you would not email it to a stranger, do not type it into a chatbot.

Slow all the way down. This is the slide that has to survive the session. Do not soften it with exceptions. A simple absolute rule everyone remembers beats a nuanced one nobody can apply under pressure at 4pm. Why it matters more here: what you type leaves CPC, and depending on the tool it may be stored. For the communities CPC serves, a status disclosure is not a privacy inconvenience. There is no written AI data rule at CPC to quote, so this slide is the rule. If someone asks whether this is policy, say exactly that: nothing is written down yet, this is the safe default until it is, and writing it down is the first thing on the open-questions slide. Do not imply it has been approved somewhere it has not — the room will find out, and you will lose the rule along with your credibility.

Ask the room

Where would the last thing you worked on go?

The most useful slide in the deck, because it is about their work rather than a generic list. Spend real time here. The lesson underneath it: the same tool is green in one department and red in another. A grant writer and an immigration caseworker are not operating under the same rules, and neither is wrong. Two worth drawing out: Budgets, yellow. It cannot reliably do arithmetic — it guesses what a calculation looks like rather than performing one. It will hand you a total that is formatted perfectly and wrong. Never trust a figure it produced; put it in a spreadsheet. Legal help, red. This is the highest-stakes column in the room. Immigration status is the single most sensitive category CPC holds, and a confident wrong answer about a filing requirement can do real damage to a real person. The green cell is deliberately narrow: explaining a term to yourself, not to a client. Then take the discussion prompt and let people bring their own. Disagreement here is useful — if two people put the same task in different columns, that is exactly the ambiguity the written policy needs to resolve. Write it down.

You can still get help

Do not

Paste the case note or the client's message and ask what to write back.

Instead

"Help me write a warm, plain-English letter telling someone their appointment has moved, and how to reach us with questions."

It does not need to know who the person is to help you write well. It almost never needs the specifics you are tempted to give it.

This slide is what stops the red light from being useless. Staff will find a way to use these tools; a rule with no workable alternative just pushes it out of sight. Good exercise if there is time: ask someone to describe a routine task out loud with no identifying detail. Harder than it sounds, and it is the skill worth practicing.

Asking well: four parts

  1. 1

    Who it is for

    "This is for a flyer for adults reading English as a second language."

  2. 2

    What you want

    "Write three short versions of a paragraph explaining what to bring."

  3. 3

    The limits

    "Under 60 words. Warm but not chatty. No jargon. Do not invent dates — leave them blank."

  4. 4

    An example

    Paste something that already sounds right and say "match this." This does more than the other three combined.

Point four is the one people skip and the one that works. If CPC already has flyers or letters in the voice they want, pasting one beats any amount of adjectives. Point three has the sleeper line: "do not invent anything, leave blanks." That converts the tool's worst habit into a to-do list. Drill it. Reminder as you say it: the example you paste must not contain client information either.

Your turn — ten minutes

Pick one real thing from your own work. Use all four parts. Then read what comes back like an editor, not an audience.

  • Rewrite a program flyer into plainer English
  • Summarize an RFP into what it actually requires
  • Draft a run-of-show for your next community event
  • Tighten a grant paragraph from 200 words to 120
  • Draft the justification text for a budget line
  • Turn last week's meeting notes into an action list

Green light only. No client information.

Circulate. The two things you will see most: one-line prompts, and people accepting the first output without reading it. Leave three or four minutes for two or three people to read theirs out. Hearing a colleague's result is more convincing than hearing yours. The six options are all real CPC work and all green. If someone wants to try something yellow, let them — but have them say out loud what they would need to check before it went anywhere. That is the more valuable exercise. The room is using Claude. Check before the day that everyone can actually sign in — if accounts are not sorted, run the exercise on the screen with the room calling out the prompt, which works nearly as well and is a better use of ten minutes than troubleshooting logins. Using Claude today is not CPC approving Claude, and the next slide still asks which tools are approved. Keep those separate out loud, or you will have accidentally announced a decision that nobody made.

What CPC has to decide

  • Which tools are approved

    Free personal accounts and paid organizational ones have very different data terms.

  • The data rule, in writing

    Today's red light is the safe default. A written rule staff have read is what holds up.

  • Who to ask when unsure

    A named person beats a policy document nobody can find.

  • Whether we say when we used it

    On grant narratives, on shared documents, with each other. Norms are easier to set now than later.

  • What gets checked before a client sees it

    Especially anything translated or anything stating a rule.

Ask the room

What would have to be true before you would trust this with something a client reads?

Have somewhere for these to go — a named owner and a follow-up date. A list of open questions with no owner is how this session becomes a nice hour that changed nothing. The disclosure question is newer than the others and worth a minute. Some funders have started asking whether AI was used in a proposal. Better for CPC to have a position than to be asked without one. The final prompt is the best closing discussion in the deck, because the answers are usually concrete and actionable — a second reader, a checklist, a named approver. Write them down; that list is the beginning of the written policy. If leadership is in the room, this is the slide to stop on.

Five things to take with you

  1. 1

    It drafts. You decide. Nothing goes out unread.

  2. 2

    No client information. Ever. Describe the situation instead of pasting it.

  3. 3

    Keep the relationship human. It can write the letter. It cannot be the conversation.

  4. 4

    Keep thinking. Read what it gives you, then form your own view. Judgment is the job.

  5. 5

    Check every fact, number, and rule at the source — not by asking it again.

Rules 1, 2, and 5 are the practical ones. Rules 3 and 4 come straight from the pillars slide, and they are the ones that will still matter in two years when the tools have changed completely. If people remember one, make it number two. If two, add number three — for an organization built on relationships with its community, that is the one with the most at stake. Offer to send these five lines round afterwards. They fit in an email, and that is where they will actually get reread.
