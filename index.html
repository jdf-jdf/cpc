Open by saying what this is not: not a policy announcement, not a tool rollout, not a decision that has been made somewhere else. It is an hour to get hands on the thing and form your own opinion. Fill in the three fields above before presenting, or delete the row.

What this hour is

We will

  • Explain what these tools actually are, without hype
  • Put you hands-on with one today
  • Be specific about what not to put in
  • Work on a real task you brought

We will not

  • Announce a policy or a tool CPC has chosen
  • Tell you AI will fix your workload
  • Ask you to use it for anything client-facing today
  • Get technical
The right-hand column matters as much as the left. Staff walk into sessions like this assuming a decision has already been made and they are being managed into it. Say plainly that no tool has been picked and nothing changes tomorrow because of this hour.

Before we start: show of hands

  1. 1 I have never used one of these
  2. 2 I tried it once and stopped
  3. 3 I use it now and then
  4. 4 I use it most weeks

There is no right answer here. This is so the session can be pitched at the room.

Actually count. If most hands go up on 1 and 2, slow down and spend longer on the demo. If most are 3 and 4, move faster through the basics and spend the time on the data rules and on checking outputs, which experienced users are usually the sloppiest about. Put your own hand up honestly too.

What an AI assistant actually is

It is a text predictor. It has read an enormous amount of writing, and when you give it text, it produces the text most likely to come next.

It is not:

  • a search engine
  • a database of facts
  • connected to CPC's files or systems
  • looking anything up, unless it has specifically been set up to
This is the single most useful idea in the session. Everything downstream — why it makes things up, why it is good at drafting and bad at facts — follows from "it predicts text." If someone asks whether it is intelligent or conscious: it is not, and you do not need to settle that debate to use the tool well. Redirect to what it does.

So it will make things up

A tool that produces plausible text produces plausible text even when it has no idea. The made-up answer looks exactly like the correct one: same confidence, same tone, same formatting.

It is not lying to you. It does not know it is wrong.

The industry word for this is "hallucination." It happens most with names, dates, numbers, citations, and rules — which is to say, with facts.

Worth a live example if you have one: ask it something specific about a small local organization or a niche rule and watch it produce a confident, wrong, well-formatted answer. That demo lands harder than any slide. Do not use a real client scenario for this.

The whole session in one line

It drafts. You decide.

Nothing it writes reaches another human being until you have read it.

Say this out loud and let it sit. Come back to it at the end. The framing that works with social services staff is professional judgment: this does not replace yours, it produces raw material for it. Same as a draft from a new intern — useful, never sent unread.

Where it genuinely helps

  • First drafts

    Routine correspondence, outreach copy, meeting summaries, agendas. Getting from a blank page to something you can react to.

  • Shortening and summarizing

    A forty-page report into a page. A long email thread into three bullets you can act on.

  • Changing the reading level

    Taking dense official language and rewriting it plainly, at the level a flyer or a form letter needs.

  • Reformatting

    Notes into a table. A list into a paragraph. One format into another, quickly.

  • Explaining jargon

    Unfamiliar terminology in a document, explained in plain English so you can decide if it matters.

  • Options when you are stuck

    Ten versions of a subject line, five ways to frame a difficult paragraph. Quantity you then judge.

Notice the pattern: it is good where you already know the answer and need help producing or reshaping text, and bad where you need it to supply the truth. Ask the room which of these six is closest to something they did this week.

Where it falls down

  • Facts and figures

    Dates, dollar amounts, statistics, citations, phone numbers. It will produce them, formatted perfectly, and they may be wrong.

  • Anything current

    Its knowledge has a cutoff, and unless the tool is searching the web it does not know about anything after it.

  • Program and benefits rules

    Eligibility thresholds, filing deadlines, documentation requirements. These change, and it does not track the change.

  • Arithmetic

    It predicts what a calculation looks like rather than performing one. Check any number that matters.

  • Knowing its own limits

    It will rarely tell you it does not know. It fills the gap instead.

  • Your context

    It has never seen CPC's programs, forms, partners, or the way your team actually works.

The benefits-rules item is the one that could actually hurt a client. If a staff member asks it what the income limit for a program is and repeats the answer to someone in intake, that is a real harm with CPC's name on it. Be blunt: it does not know your programs, and it will answer anyway.

The one rule to take out of this room

Client information never goes in

  • Names, addresses, phone numbers, dates of birth
  • Immigration or citizenship status
  • Case notes and anything from a file
  • Benefits, income, or household details
  • Medical or mental health information
  • Photographs of documents

If you would not email it to a stranger, do not type it into a chatbot.

Slow all the way down here. This is the slide that has to survive the session. Do not soften it with exceptions. A simple absolute rule that everyone remembers beats a nuanced one that nobody can apply under pressure at 4pm. If CPC later approves a tool with an enterprise agreement, the rule can be revisited then. Until that exists in writing, this is the rule.

Why this matters more at CPC than most places

When you type into a public AI tool, that text leaves CPC. Depending on the product and its settings, it may be retained, and it may be readable by staff at that company.

CPC serves immigrant communities. A disclosure of someone's status is not a privacy inconvenience. Treat that data the way the people it describes would want it treated.

CPC's own confidentiality and data policies govern this, not this slide. Confirm what those say before presenting.

Check with whoever owns data policy at CPC before you run the session, and quote their language rather than yours. If there is an existing confidentiality policy staff already signed, name it — this then becomes a familiar rule applied to a new tool rather than a new rule to learn. If no policy exists yet, say that plainly and put it on the open-questions slide.

You can still get help. Describe the situation.

Do not

Paste the case note, the intake form, or the client's message and ask it what to write back.

Instead

"Help me write a warm, plain-English letter telling someone their appointment has been rescheduled and how to reach us with questions."

The tool does not need to know who the person is to help you write well. It almost never needs the specifics you are tempted to give it.

This slide is what stops the rule from being useless. Staff will find a way to use these tools; a rule with no workable alternative just pushes it out of sight. Good exercise if there is time: ask someone to describe a routine task out loud without any identifying detail. It is harder than it sounds and it is the skill worth practicing.

Asking well: four parts

  1. 1

    Context

    Who you are and who this is for. "I work at a community organization. This is for a flyer for adults reading in a second language."

  2. 2

    Task

    The actual thing you want, stated plainly. "Write three versions of a short paragraph explaining what to bring to an appointment."

  3. 3

    Constraints

    Length, tone, reading level, what to avoid. "Under 60 words. Warm but not chatty. No jargon."

  4. 4

    An example

    Paste something that already sounds right. This does more than the other three combined.

Point four is the one people skip and the one that works. If CPC already has flyers or letters written in the voice they want, pasting one and saying "match this tone" gets further than any amount of adjectives. Reminder as you say it: the example you paste must not contain client information either.

The same request, twice

Thin

Write a flyer about our computer class.

Produces something generic, over-enthusiastic, and full of exclamation marks. You end up rewriting it entirely.

Worth sending

I work at a community organization in New York. Write a short flyer paragraph for a free beginners computer class. Readers are adults, many reading English as a second language. Under 70 words, plain sentences, warm but direct, no exclamation marks. Do not invent any dates or locations — leave those blank for me to fill in.

The last sentence is the important one. Told to leave gaps, it leaves gaps instead of inventing details.

Run both live if the room has a screen. The contrast does the teaching. "Do not invent anything, leave blanks" is a habit worth drilling. It converts the tool's worst failure mode into a to-do list.

Your turn — ten minutes

Pick one real thing from your own work. Write a prompt with all four parts. Read what comes back like an editor, not an audience.

  • Rewrite something official into plain English
  • Summarize a long document you never finished reading
  • Draft an agenda for a meeting you are running
  • Turn messy notes into a clean list
  • Get ten subject lines for an email you have been avoiding

No client information. Describe, do not paste.

Circulate. The two things you will see most: prompts that are one line long, and people accepting the first output without reading it. Leave three or four minutes at the end for two or three people to read theirs out. Hearing a colleague's result is more convincing than hearing yours. Decide in advance which tool the room is using and whether accounts are needed. Open question — see the last slide.

Checking what it gave you

  1. Every fact, at the source

    Dates, amounts, names, statistics, links. Not by asking the tool whether it is sure — it will simply agree with you.

  2. Every rule, with the person who owns it

    Anything about eligibility, deadlines, or documentation goes to the program staff or legal, every time.

  3. Read it aloud

    Does it sound like CPC, or like a brochure? These tools drift toward corporate enthusiasm unless told not to.

  4. Ask what is missing

    It answers what you asked. It will not tell you that you asked the wrong question.

The first point is worth dwelling on. Asking "are you sure?" is the most common checking method and the most useless one — it will apologize and produce a different answer with the same confidence, which is not verification.

Translation, specifically

It is fast and often good for gist — understanding roughly what an email says, or getting a rough first pass a bilingual colleague then fixes.

It is not sufficient on its own for:

  • Legal notices and anything a client signs
  • Benefits or eligibility determinations
  • Medical instructions
  • Anything CPC's language-access obligations cover

It also tends toward a formal register that may not match how a community actually speaks. A fluent translation can still be the wrong one.

This slide will get pushback from anyone under deadline pressure, because the tool is genuinely useful here and the temptation is real. Do not overclaim — say clearly that rough-pass-then-human-review is legitimate, and that the human review is not optional. CPC's own language-access requirements are the authority. Check them before presenting.

What CPC has to decide

  • Which tools are approved

    Free consumer accounts and paid organizational accounts have very different data terms.

  • What the data rule is, in writing

    Today's rule is the safe default. A written one that staff have actually read is what holds up.

  • Who to ask when unsure

    A named person beats a policy document nobody can find.

  • What gets reviewed before a client sees it

    Especially anything translated or anything stating a rule.

  • Whether to share what works

    Prompts that work well for CPC's writing are worth collecting rather than everyone rediscovering them.

None of these are answered here. This session does not set policy.

Have somewhere for these to go — a named owner and a follow-up date. A list of open questions with no owner is how this session becomes a nice hour that changed nothing. If leadership is in the room, this is the slide to stop on.

Four things to take with you

  1. 1

    It predicts text. It will be confidently wrong, and wrong looks exactly like right.

  2. 2

    It drafts, you decide. Nothing goes out unread.

  3. 3

    No client information, ever. Describe the situation instead of pasting it.

  4. 4

    Verify every fact, number, and rule with the source that owns it.

If people remember one thing, make it number three. If they remember two, add number one. Offer to send the four lines round afterwards — they fit in an email and that is where they will actually get reread.
