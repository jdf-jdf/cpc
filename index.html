Learn to use AI, starting from zero.

A proposed introduction to AI for adults who have never opened one of these tools, and for anyone who tried once, got a strange answer, and stopped. The draft goal: leave able to use AI for things you already do — writing a letter, reading a form, looking for work.

Headline and framing are a proposal. The program itself — length, cost, schedule, who runs it — is undecided.

Who a class like this would serve

A proposed audience definition, offered as a starting point. CPC staff know these communities far better than this draft does, and this is the part most worth arguing with.

  • People who have never used an AI tool

    They have heard about it from their children, their co-workers, or the news, and have never tried it.

  • People who use a phone, not a computer

    Texting, WeChat or WhatsApp, photos. Whether the class teaches on laptops, phones, or both is undecided.

  • People whose first language is not English

    Whether instruction or interpretation is offered, and in which languages, is an open question.

  • People who want this for work

    Job hunting, running a small business, or keeping up with tools their workplace has started using.

  • People worried about being fooled

    Fake voices, fake photos, and scams that target these communities specifically. A strong candidate for its own session.

  • A defined eligibility group

    Age, residency, income, and referral requirements are not defined and should follow whatever CPC's funding requires.

A draft curriculum, for discussion

Six proposed sessions, sketched to show what the page looks like with real content in it. The number of sessions, their length, and the topics are all a proposal — not a syllabus anyone has approved.

  1. 1

    What AI actually is, and what it is not

    A plain explanation with no jargon: what these tools do, where the answers come from, and why they sometimes make things up. Ideally everyone types a first question and gets a first answer in session one.

  2. 2

    Writing things people actually have to write

    A letter to a landlord about a repair. A note to a child's teacher. A message to an employer. Participants bring the situation and leave with the letter, including how to make the tool sound like them rather than like a robot.

  3. 3

    Reading letters, forms, and notices

    Photographing a document and having it explained. Where AI translation is genuinely useful, and the categories — legal, medical, immigration — where it should never be the last word. CPC's own guidance should shape this session.

  4. 4

    Looking for work

    Turning a job posting into plain language, building a resume from a work history described out loud, and practicing interview answers. This is the session most likely to need alignment with CPC's existing workforce programs.

  5. 5

    Not getting scammed

    Cloned voices, fake photos, fake job offers, and messages that appear to come from a family member. What current scams look like, how to check before acting, and what should never be typed into a chatbot.

  6. 6

    Leaving with something that works

    Setting up tools on a participant's own device before they leave the room. What ongoing support exists after the last session is undecided, and it may be the single biggest factor in whether any of this sticks.

What has to be decided

These are the questions that determine whether people actually show up and finish. Every one of them is a real cost or staffing decision, and none of them is answered here. The page has a place for each answer once it exists.

  • Is it free, and who pays?

    Cost to the participant, and the funding source behind it. Everything else on this page depends on the answer.

  • Do participants need their own device?

    Whether the program can lend laptops, teach on phones people already own, or use a computer lab at a site.

  • What language support is offered?

    Interpretation, bilingual instructors, or translated materials — and in which languages. This drives both staffing and the page's translation needs.

  • What removes the barriers to attending?

    Transit help, childcare, meals, evening or weekend timing. Whichever of these CPC can fund belongs on the page; whichever it cannot should not be implied.

  • Is there a credential at the end?

    A certificate, an attendance requirement to earn it, and whether it connects to anything CPC or an employer recognizes.

  • Who answers the phone?

    Which staff or site handles sign-ups, and what happens to the information collected. The page should not state a privacy practice CPC has not set.
