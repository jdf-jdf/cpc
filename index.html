What this hour is

We will

  • Explain what these tools are, in plain English
  • Put you hands-on with one today
  • Be clear about what not to put in
  • Try it on a real task you brought

We will not

  • Announce a policy or a tool CPC has picked
  • Tell you AI will fix your workload
  • Ask you to use it with client information
  • Get technical
The right column matters as much as the left. Staff walk into sessions like this assuming a decision has already been made and they are being managed into it. Say plainly that no tool has been picked and nothing changes tomorrow because of this hour.

Show of hands

  1. 1 Never used one
  2. 2 Tried it once, stopped
  3. 3 Use it now and then
  4. 4 Use it most weeks

No right answer. This is so the session can be pitched at the room.

Actually count. Mostly 1s and 2s: slow down, spend longer on the demo. Mostly 3s and 4s: move faster through the basics and spend the time on the red light and on checking outputs, which experienced users are usually sloppiest about. Put your own hand up honestly too.

What is a chatbot?

You type a question. It types back an answer. That is the whole thing.

Underneath, it is guessing what words come next — based on having read an enormous amount of writing. So it is not:

  • searching the internet
  • looking in a database
  • connected to CPC's files
  • checking whether it is right
Keep this slide short and land the one idea: it predicts text. Everything on the next few slides follows from it. If someone asks whether it is intelligent or conscious: you do not need to settle that to use the tool well. Move on. A useful line: extremely well read, extremely fast, and sometimes confidently wrong.

So it makes things up

Something that guesses the next words will guess even when it does not know. The made-up answer looks exactly like a correct one: same confidence, same tone, same neat formatting.

It is not lying. It does not know it is wrong.

It happens most with names, dates, numbers, and rules — which is to say, with facts.

Worth a live example if you have one: ask it something specific about a small local organization or a niche rule and watch it produce a confident, wrong, well-formatted answer. That demo lands harder than the slide. Do not use a real client scenario for this.

The whole session in one line

It drafts. You decide.

Nothing it writes reaches another person until you have read it.

Say it out loud and let it sit. Come back to it at the end. The framing that works with social services staff is professional judgment: this does not replace yours. It produces raw material for it, like a draft from a new intern — useful, never sent unread.

Three ways you will meet it

  • 1

    Chat

    You type, it types back. One window. It cannot open your files or do anything on your computer.

    Everyone starts here

  • 2

    Cowork

    It works on your actual documents. Reads and writes files, connects to things like Google Drive, and carries a multi-step job through to a finished deliverable.

    Built for the work most of this room does

  • 3

    Code

    It works inside software projects, in a developer's terminal, writing and changing code.

    Built for engineers

Same underlying thing. The difference is how much it is allowed to touch — and the more it can touch, the more the rules later in this session matter.

The point of this slide is that "AI" is not one thing, and the version people have heard about (a chat window) is the least capable of the three. Cowork is Anthropic's product for knowledge work — research, document preparation, file work — and it is the category that maps to what CPC program and admin staff actually do. Code is for engineers; mention it and move on so nobody thinks this is a coding session. Whether CPC would use any of these, and which, is an open question — say so.

The line that lands: you would not hire a PhD to alphabetize a list. For your own reference, Anthropic's current tiers run roughly Haiku (fast and cheap), Sonnet (the balanced default), Opus (heavy reasoning), and Fable (the most capable). Deliberately not on the slide — names change faster than a deck does, and this room does not need them. If someone asks which one they should use: whichever the tool gives them by default is almost always fine to start.

You can also tell it how hard to think

Same helper. Two different instructions.

"Give me a quick answer"

  • Answers in seconds
  • Costs less
  • Fine for routine work
  • Thinner on hard problems

"Take your time and think"

  • Takes longer — sometimes minutes
  • Costs more
  • Catches more on complicated work
  • Can overthink something simple

Rule of thumb: quick for routine, careful for anything that matters. Most tools have a setting for this, or you can just ask in plain words.

This is the one genuinely new concept for people who have used a chatbot before, and it is worth the minute. Most people do not know the setting exists. In practice the two dials interact: a mid-tier helper told to think carefully often beats a top-tier one told to answer fast, and costs less. If someone asks how to choose, that is the honest answer. Do not let this become a technical detour. If questions get deep, take them offline.

Green light

Go ahead. No permission needed.

  • Rewriting something official into plain English
  • Summarizing a long document you never finished
  • A first draft of routine writing
  • Turning messy notes into a clean list
  • Brainstorming when you are stuck
  • Explaining jargon you ran into

None of these involve a real person's information.

Say explicitly that green means green — staff do not need to ask anyone before doing these. A training that only lists prohibitions teaches people the tool is a liability and they quietly use it anyway. Ask which of the six someone did this week. Usually several hands.

Yellow light

Use it, then check before it goes anywhere.

  • Anything with a date, number, or dollar amount
  • Anything about program rules or who qualifies
  • Translation — fine for gist, not for anything binding
  • Anything a client will read
  • Anything leaving CPC with our name on it
  • Anything you would sign your name to

The tool drafts. A person checks. Every time.

The checking method matters: verify at the source, not by asking the tool whether it is sure. Asked "are you sure?", it will apologize and produce a different answer with the same confidence. That is not verification. Translation gets pushback from anyone under deadline pressure, because the tool is genuinely useful there. Do not overclaim: rough pass then human review is legitimate; the human review is not optional. CPC's language-access obligations govern, not this slide.

Red light — the one rule to take out of this room

Client information never goes in

  • Names, addresses, phone numbers, dates of birth
  • Immigration or citizenship status
  • Case notes, or anything from a file
  • Benefits, income, or household details
  • Medical or mental health information
  • Photographs of documents

If you would not email it to a stranger, do not type it into a chatbot.

Slow all the way down. This is the slide that has to survive the session. Do not soften it with exceptions. A simple absolute rule everyone remembers beats a nuanced one nobody can apply under pressure at 4pm. Why it matters more here: what you type leaves CPC, and depending on the tool it may be stored. For the communities CPC serves, a status disclosure is not a privacy inconvenience. CPC's own confidentiality policy governs — check what it says before presenting and quote their language rather than this slide's.

You can still get help

Do not

Paste the case note or the client's message and ask what to write back.

Instead

"Help me write a warm, plain-English letter telling someone their appointment has moved, and how to reach us with questions."

It does not need to know who the person is to help you write well. It almost never needs the specifics you are tempted to give it.

This slide is what stops the red light from being useless. Staff will find a way to use these tools; a rule with no workable alternative just pushes it out of sight. Good exercise if there is time: ask someone to describe a routine task out loud with no identifying detail. Harder than it sounds, and it is the skill worth practicing.

Asking well: four parts

  1. 1

    Who it is for

    "This is for a flyer for adults reading English as a second language."

  2. 2

    What you want

    "Write three short versions of a paragraph explaining what to bring."

  3. 3

    The limits

    "Under 60 words. Warm but not chatty. No jargon. Do not invent dates — leave them blank."

  4. 4

    An example

    Paste something that already sounds right and say "match this." This does more than the other three combined.

Point four is the one people skip and the one that works. If CPC already has flyers or letters in the voice they want, pasting one beats any amount of adjectives. Point three has the sleeper line: "do not invent anything, leave blanks." That converts the tool's worst habit into a to-do list. Drill it. Reminder as you say it: the example you paste must not contain client information either.

Your turn — ten minutes

Pick one real thing from your own work. Use all four parts. Then read what comes back like an editor, not an audience.

  • Rewrite something official into plain English
  • Summarize a long document
  • Draft an agenda for a meeting you are running
  • Turn messy notes into a clean list
  • Get ten subject lines for an email you have been avoiding

Green light tasks only. No client information.

Circulate. The two things you will see most: one-line prompts, and people accepting the first output without reading it. Leave three or four minutes for two or three people to read theirs out. Hearing a colleague's result is more convincing than hearing yours. Decide in advance which tool the room uses and whether accounts are needed. Open question — see the next slide.

What CPC has to decide

  • Which tools are approved

    Free personal accounts and paid organizational ones have very different data terms.

  • The data rule, in writing

    Today's red light is the safe default. A written rule staff have read is what holds up.

  • Who to ask when unsure

    A named person beats a policy document nobody can find.

  • What gets checked before a client sees it

    Especially anything translated or anything stating a rule.

  • Whether to share what works

    Prompts that work well for CPC's writing are worth collecting once, not rediscovering ten times.

None of these are answered here. This session does not set policy.

Have somewhere for these to go — a named owner and a follow-up date. A list of open questions with no owner is how this session becomes a nice hour that changed nothing. If leadership is in the room, this is the slide to stop on.

Four things to take with you

  1. 1

    It guesses the next words. It will be confidently wrong, and wrong looks exactly like right.

  2. 2

    It drafts, you decide. Nothing goes out unread.

  3. 3

    No client information. Ever. Describe the situation instead.

  4. 4

    Green, yellow, red. When in doubt, it is not green.

If people remember one thing, make it number three. If two, add number one. Offer to send these four lines round afterwards — they fit in an email, and that is where they will actually get reread.
