AI Basics

A working session for CPC staff

Presenter
Jo Flores
Open by saying what this is not: not a policy announcement, not a tool rollout, not a decision made somewhere else. It is an hour to get hands on the thing and form your own opinion. This deck is built for a small group and a conversation. Four places are marked for discussion — the icebreaker, and three "Ask the room" prompts. In a room of eight, those are the parts worth protecting when you run short on time; the slides can be skimmed, the conversation cannot be recovered later. There is no date on the slide on purpose, so the deck can be presented more than once without editing it.

What this hour is

We will

  • What these tools actually are
  • What we want to protect
  • What is safe, what is not
  • Hands on your own work

We will not

  • Announce a policy or a tool
  • Promise it fixes your workload
  • Touch client information
  • Get technical
The right column matters as much as the left. Staff walk into sessions like this assuming a decision has already been made and they are being managed into it. Say plainly that no tool has been picked and nothing changes tomorrow because of this hour.

Over to you

What is the most embarrassing thing you have used AI for?

A birthday message · Getting out of plans · Naming a pet · Arguing with a landlord

You go first, and make it genuinely a little embarrassing. If you do not, nobody will. The examples on the slide are there to prime the pump if the room stalls — read one out rather than letting silence do the work. What this is actually for: almost everyone in the room is already using these tools and almost nobody has said so out loud at work. Once that is in the open, the rest of the hour is a conversation between people who all use it, rather than a lecture at people who supposedly do not. If you want a quick read on the room first, ask for hands: never used one, tried it once, use it now and then, use it most weeks. Twenty seconds, and it tells you whether to slow down on the basics or move through them. If the room is quiet, offer two or three of your own until someone bites. In a small group this usually takes one. Watch for the person who admits something that breaks the red-light rule later in the deck — pasting a client's message, uploading a document. Do not make an example of them. Thank them, move on, and let slide 16 do the work. If you embarrass the one person honest enough to say it, nobody tells you anything for the rest of the session, and you lose the only real information you were going to get.

What is a chatbot?

You type. It types back.

Always

  • Guesses the next words
  • Does not check whether it is right

Sometimes

  • Searches the web
  • Reads a document you give it
  • Reaches files it has been connected to

Where did this come from? Ask every time.

Keep this short and land the one idea: it predicts text. Everything on the next few slides follows from it. Say the right-hand column out loud, because the slide only lists it. Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and Copilot can all do all three. Whether they do depends on the tool, on what has been connected to it, and on what was asked — and some of it happens without being asked at all. The right-hand column is why this slide is not the simple one it looks like. Two years ago the left column was the whole picture — these things could not look anything up. That is no longer true of any of the four, and a deck that says otherwise loses the first person in the room who has watched ChatGPT search the web. The practical version, and the reason it is worth thirty seconds: "it cannot see CPC's files" is not a safety guarantee anybody should be relying on. If CPC switches on Copilot inside Microsoft 365, or connects a shared drive, then it can. The red-light rule later is about what you type into it and what has been plugged into it, and the second half is not a decision most staff will be making themselves. The habit to teach is one question: where did this come from? If it hands you a fact, ask whether it looked that up or guessed it. Most tools will tell you, and a link is the thing to look for. Do not explain how any of the looking-up works. Nobody in the room needs it and it costs you the slide. If someone asks whether it is intelligent or conscious: you do not need to settle that to use the tool well. Move on. A useful line: extremely well read, extremely fast, and sometimes confidently wrong.

Very fancy predictive text

The community meeting starts at

  • seven
  • noon
  • 6:30

Your phone: the last few words. This: most of the internet.

As good as what it read

Textbooks. Journals. A lot of Reddit.

Plays whatever part you give it

“You are a grant writer for a community nonprofit.”

The line that makes this land: your phone guesses from the last three words, this guesses from nearly everything ever written down. Same trick, enormously more of it. Point at the highlighted guess. "Noon" is the likeliest word, not the true one — the meeting may well start at 6:30. That is the whole of the next slide, handed to you for free, so do not spend it here. Say the parts the slide only gestures at. Nothing on the screen tells you which half of what it read you just got. And predicting words means predicting a role — that one line of framing changes every word that comes after it. The Reddit joke is the teaching, not the decoration. It is how you tell someone how much to trust an answer that arrives with no source attached. Land it and move on — do not let it become a rant about the internet. If someone pushes back that it is more than Reddit, they are right and you should say so. These are also trained on licensed and edited writing, and tuned heavily afterwards. The point is not that it is worthless. The point is that nothing on the screen tells you which part of it you are getting, so you check. The role-playing half is the more useful half, and it is the one nobody arrives knowing. Everything on the "Asking well" slide later is this idea made practical: you are not typing a question, you are setting up who is answering. Say the shadow side out loud, because it sets up the next slide: it will play "immigration attorney" just as willingly, and just as convincingly, with no idea whether it is any good at it. Playing a part is not the same as being one. Do not use "it is just autocomplete" as a put-down. It is a genuinely useful tool and the room will stop listening to the parts where you say so. If you have a phone you can put on the screen, type "the meeting starts at" into Messages and let them watch the suggestions appear. Ten seconds, and it does the whole slide.

It is really good at improv

The first rule of improv: never turn down an offer.

  • Ask for a case number → you get a case number
  • Ask which rule applies → you get a rule
  • Same confident tone either way

It is not lying. It is staying in character.

Ask the room

Has one of these ever given you an answer you knew was wrong? What was it?

It never runs out of next words. That is the sentence the slide leaves to you, and it is the reason any of the rest follows. The improv framing is doing real work here, so do not rush past it. "It lies" makes it sound malicious and "it is broken" makes it sound fixable. Neither is true. An improviser who invents a case number is not lying to you — they are doing precisely what the form asks, which is to keep the scene going. So is this. The sentence to land: it has no move for "I do not know." That is not a flaw it will grow out of, it is the shape of the thing. Worth a live example if you have one: ask it something specific about a small local organization or a niche rule and watch it produce a confident, wrong, well-formatted answer. That demo lands harder than the slide. Do not use a real client scenario for this. If someone says it has told them it was not sure — true, they are trained to hedge, and the hedging helps. But the hedge is predicted text like everything else. It appears when uncertainty is the likely next thing to say, not when the tool has checked. Do not let the room start treating confidence as a signal. The discussion prompt usually surfaces something better than anything you could have prepared, because it comes from their own work. Let two or three people answer. Where this hurts most: names, dates, numbers, and rules. Which is to say, facts. Flag that it comes back on the budget line of the work map.

The whole session in one line

It drafts. You decide.

Nothing it writes reaches another person until you have read it.

Say it out loud and let it sit. Come back to it at the end. The framing that works with social services staff is professional judgment: this does not replace yours. It produces raw material for it, like a draft from a new intern — useful, never sent unread.

Five pillars, from the Center for Humane Technology.

Everything after this slide — the rules, the lights, the checking — exists to protect these five. Say that: it is not compliance. Then fill in the lines the slide does not carry. Stop thinking about a case because something summarised it and you got worse at your work, not faster. These tools shape what you come to believe over time. The faking applies to our community as much as to us. And the worry about being replaced is worth naming out loud rather than managing around. This is the slide that gives the rest of the hour its reason. Without it, the traffic lights read as CPC covering itself. With it, they read as protecting the thing CPC is. The five pillars are the Center for Humane Technology's, from their 2026 report "Preserving What Makes Us Deeply Human in the Age of AI." Their originals: relationships, cognitive capacities, inner worlds, identities, work and contributions. The one-line translations here are ours, written for this room. Credit them — they are worth reading and the report is short. Their central argument, worth saying out loud: these harms are not accidents or growing pains. They come from business models that optimize for engagement and dependency, the same pattern that played out with social media. That framing usually changes how a room hears everything that follows. Pillar 1 is the one to dwell on for CPC. An organization whose entire value is human relationships with immigrant and low-income communities has more at stake here than a company selling software. Say that plainly. Pillar 5 is the one people are quietly worried about. Naming it costs you nothing and buys you the room's attention for the rest of the hour. Do not promise anything you cannot promise.

Over to you

Which of those five feels most at risk in your work?

Relationships · Thinking · Inner lives · Identities · Work

The best five minutes in the deck. Let it run. Expect relationships and work to dominate — those are the two that touch CPC staff most directly. If someone raises thinking, that is the sophisticated answer and worth drawing out: the risk is not that the tool is wrong, it is that you stop checking. Do not resolve the discussion. You are not here to reassure anyone. The honest position is that these are real risks, this room does not control most of them, and the part it does control is how CPC chooses to use the thing — which is the rest of the session. You can also tell it how hard to think

Same helper. Two different instructions.

"Give me a quick answer"

  • Answers in seconds
  • Costs less
  • Fine for routine work
  • Thinner on hard problems

"Take your time and think"

  • Takes longer — sometimes minutes
  • Costs more
  • Catches more on complicated work
  • Can overthink something simple

Quick for routine. Careful for anything that matters.

This is the one genuinely new concept for people who have used a chatbot before, and it is worth the minute. Most people do not know the setting exists. In practice the two dials interact: a mid-tier helper told to think carefully often beats a top-tier one told to answer fast, and costs less. If someone asks how to choose, that is the honest answer. Do not let this become a technical detour. If questions get deep, take them offline.

Green light

Go ahead. No permission needed.

  • Rewriting something official into plain English
  • Summarizing a long document you never finished
  • A first draft of routine writing
  • Turning messy notes into a clean list
  • Brainstorming when you are stuck
  • Explaining jargon you ran into

None of these involve a real person's information.

Say explicitly that green means green — staff do not need to ask anyone before doing these. A training that only lists prohibitions teaches people the tool is a liability and they quietly use it anyway. Ask which of the six someone did this week. Usually several hands.

Yellow light

Use it, then check before it goes anywhere.

  • Anything with a date, number, or dollar amount
  • Anything about program rules or who qualifies
  • Translation — fine for gist, not for anything binding
  • Anything a client will read
  • Anything leaving CPC with our name on it
  • Anything you would sign your name to

The tool drafts. A person checks. Every time.

The checking method matters: verify at the source, not by asking the tool whether it is sure. Asked "are you sure?", it will apologize and produce a different answer with the same confidence. That is not verification. Translation gets pushback from anyone under deadline pressure, because the tool is genuinely useful there. Do not overclaim: rough pass then human review is legitimate; the human review is not optional. CPC's language-access obligations govern, not this slide.

Red light — the one rule to take out of this room

Client information never goes in

  • Names, addresses, phone numbers, dates of birth
  • Immigration or citizenship status
  • Case notes, or anything from a file
  • Benefits, income, or household details
  • Medical or mental health information
  • Photographs of documents

If you would not email it to a stranger, do not type it into a chatbot.

Slow all the way down. This is the slide that has to survive the session. Do not soften it with exceptions. A simple absolute rule everyone remembers beats a nuanced one nobody can apply under pressure at 4pm. Why it matters more here: what you type leaves CPC, and depending on the tool it may be stored. For the communities CPC serves, a status disclosure is not a privacy inconvenience. There is no written AI data rule at CPC to quote, so this slide is the rule. If someone asks whether this is policy, say exactly that: nothing is written down yet, this is the safe default until it is, and writing it down is the first thing on the open-questions slide. Do not imply it has been approved somewhere it has not — the room will find out, and you will lose the rule along with your credibility.

Ask the room

Where would the last thing you worked on go?

The most useful slide in the deck, because it is about their work rather than a generic list. Spend real time here. The lesson underneath it: the same tool is green in one department and red in another. A grant writer and an immigration caseworker are not operating under the same rules, and neither is wrong. Two worth drawing out: Budgets, yellow. It cannot reliably do arithmetic — it guesses what a calculation looks like rather than performing one. It will hand you a total that is formatted perfectly and wrong. Never trust a figure it produced; put it in a spreadsheet. Legal help, red. This is the highest-stakes column in the room. Immigration status is the single most sensitive category CPC holds, and a confident wrong answer about a filing requirement can do real damage to a real person. The green cell is deliberately narrow: explaining a term to yourself, not to a client. Then take the discussion prompt and let people bring their own. Disagreement here is useful — if two people put the same task in different columns, that is exactly the ambiguity the written policy needs to resolve. Write it down.

On your paper

Draw a tree.

Thirty seconds. No talking, and do not look at anyone else’s.

Hand the paper out before you put this slide up, so nobody is reading ahead while you are still distributing. Thirty seconds, and mean it. The worse the drawings the better this works, so say out loud that nobody is being judged on draughtsmanship. Do not explain the point. The entire exercise dies if you tell them what it is for before they do it. Someone will ask what kind of tree. That is the experiment. "Draw a tree" is the whole instruction — say that pleasantly and let the ambiguity sit there.

Now

I meant a lemon tree.

Same paper. Thirty seconds.

Do not say whether to add to the drawing they have or start a new one. What they choose is the data you use on the takeaway slide, and telling them ruins it. Watch the room while they draw. Nearly everyone adds lemons to the tree already in front of them. Remember that, because you are going to ask about it in two slides.

Last one

I meant a lemon tree in a pot.

Same paper. Then hold it up.

Get everyone to hold their paper up at the same moment, not one at a time. Eight drawings in the air at once is the exercise; going around the room one by one is a show and tell, and it takes four times as long. Look at them yourself and say what you see out loud — an oak, a palm, a Christmas tree, a stick with a blob on top. Name a few. It is funnier from you than from them, and it saves anyone having to volunteer that theirs is bad.

    You did not describe the whole tree again.

  3. 3In a pot

    Three rounds, one picture.

You each filled the gap with your own default. So does it.

The line the slide no longer spells out, and worth saying: nothing you drew was wrong. You answered a vaguer question than the one in my head, the way the tool answers a vaguer question than the one in yours — and it fills the gap with whatever was most ordinary in everything it has read. Two questions, in this order. They are the whole slide. First: how many different trees are in this room? Get them to say the kinds out loud. Then the point — none of you was wrong, you each had a default and you used it. That is exactly what the tool does. Its default is just whatever was most common in what it read. Second: at step two, did you add to the tree you had, or start a new drawing? Nearly everyone adds. That is context. You did not need the whole description again because the conversation was already carrying it, and neither does the tool. This is the half people who think of it as a search box have never met. Then the bridge, which is the next slide: three rounds got you to a potted lemon tree. One sentence would have too. Rounds are a perfectly good way to work — but if you already know what you want, say it once and skip two rounds. If somebody drew a potted lemon tree at step one, hold it up. It is funny, and it is the lesson: they read more into the instruction than was there, and this time it happened to match. That is luck, not accuracy.

Specific does not get you one answer. It gets you a smaller range.

The kicker is the line to land, and it is the one people have not heard before: you are not aiming at a target, you are narrowing a field. The field never shrinks to a point. That is also why asking well is not a set of magic words. It is a decision about how much of the field you are willing to leave open, made fresh every time. Tie the two exercises together here, because they teach opposite halves of the same thing. The paper showed you that both you and the tool fill an unspecified gap with your own default. This showed you that closing gaps narrows the range without ever closing it. The next slide is how to close as much of it as you actually care about. Someone will ask how specific is specific enough. The honest answer: you stop when the range left over is one you would be happy with any answer from. That is a judgement call, it is theirs to make, and it is different for a flyer than for anything a client reads.

Asking well: four parts

  1. 1

    Who it is for

    "This is for a flyer for adults reading English as a second language."

  2. 2

    What you want

    "Write three short versions of a paragraph explaining what to bring."

  3. 3

    The limits

    "Under 60 words. Warm but not chatty. No jargon. Do not invent dates — leave them blank."

  4. 4

    An example

    Paste something that already sounds right: “match this.”

Point four is the one people skip and the one that works. If CPC already has flyers or letters in the voice they want, pasting one beats any amount of adjectives. Point three has the sleeper line: "do not invent anything, leave blanks." That converts the tool's worst habit into a to-do list. Drill it. Reminder as you say it: the example you paste must not contain client information either.

Ask the room

What would have to be true before you would trust this with something a client reads?

Have somewhere for these to go — a named owner and a follow-up date. A list of open questions with no owner is how this session becomes a nice hour that changed nothing. Five things to take with you

  1. 1

    It drafts. You decide. Nothing goes out unread.

  2. 2

    No client information. Ever. Describe the situation instead of pasting it.

  3. 3

    Keep the relationship human. It can write the letter. It cannot be the conversation.

  4. 4

    Keep thinking. Read what it gives you, then form your own view. Judgment is the job.

  5. 5

    Check every fact, number, and rule at the source — not by asking it again.

Rules 1, 2, and 5 are the practical ones. Rules 3 and 4 come straight from the pillars slide, and they are the ones that will still matter in two years when the tools have changed completely. If people remember one, make it number two. If two, add number three — for an organization built on relationships with its community, that is the one with the most at stake. Offer to send these five lines round afterwards. They fit in an email, and that is where they will actually get reread.
